Structural Models × AI

Structural models and AI can work together because economic structure does not require a rigid functional form. A model can retain assumptions about choice, information, and equilibrium while using machine learning to measure its inputs, estimate flexible functions, or make computation feasible.

My coursework on machine learning and market counterfactuals has shaped how I think about this combination. Economic assumptions define the objects I want to learn and the changes I want to evaluate. Machine learning provides tools for representing and estimating those objects. Predictive accuracy alone does not establish identification or justify a policy counterfactual.

The directions below connect these methods to my interests in demand, consumer learning, and decision making.

Text and images as inputs to economic models

Reviews and product descriptions contain information that is difficult to capture in a conventional dataset. NLP and learned embeddings can turn that information into measures of product attributes or consumer experiences. A demand or learning model can then connect these measures to choices and substitution patterns. Compiani, Morozov, and Seiler provide a concrete example by incorporating text and image embeddings into a mixed logit demand model.

In my automobile-review project with Sha Yang, this connection begins with text measures checked against star ratings. A direction I would like to pursue is allowing uncertainty in those measures to carry through to demand estimates and information counterfactuals.

Related reading: Compiani, Morozov, and Seiler.

Flexible estimation with economic identification

Machine learning can estimate nonlinear relationships while an economic model retains the restrictions that identify its parameters. For example, ML can learn a first stage from valid instruments, or neural networks can approximate unknown functions in nonparametric instrumental-variable models. The instruments and conditional moment restrictions still do the identification work; flexibility alone does not remove endogeneity.

I am interested in how these tools can improve demand estimation with rich covariates and imperfect measurement. Sample splitting, regularization, and inference that accounts for estimated functions are essential to making the resulting economic conclusions credible.

Related readings: Chen, Chen, and Lewis; Chen, Chen, and Tamer; Farrell, Liang, and Misra.

Neural networks inside dynamic models

Dynamic models can be expensive to solve when firms or consumers face many possible states. Neural networks can approximate costly parts of the computation while the model keeps its decision rules and Bellman conditions. In the preschool-market example discussed in class, Bodéré trains a network to approximate static equilibrium profits and uses its learned features in value-function approximation.

This suggests a possible extension of my work on consumer learning: approximate continuation values or other expensive model components, then check the approximation against the model’s optimality conditions and the states reached under a counterfactual. Reinforcement learning also connects to dynamic decision problems, but using it as a solution method is different from assuming that people themselves learn through an RL algorithm.

Related reading: Bodéré.

Flexible supply and market counterfactuals

The market-counterfactual lecture gives a direct example of economic structure combined with ML. Chiang, Collison, Magnolfi, and Sullivan use neural networks and the Variational Method of Moments to estimate a flexible supply relationship, with instruments addressing endogenous market outcomes. Counterfactual prices must still be consistent with demand and market equilibrium.

I would like to understand how this flexibility changes the conclusions of merger, tax, or product-regulation exercises relative to conventional supply specifications. It also makes the limits clear: the combined supply relationship does not by itself separate marginal costs from markups, and counterfactuals far beyond the observed data require particular care.

Related reading: Chiang, Collison, Magnolfi, and Sullivan.

Selected readings

Papers discussed in my coursework that connect to these ideas. Links point to published articles or author-hosted versions; some working papers have been revised since the lectures.

  1. Demand Estimation with Text and Image Data
    Giovanni Compiani, Ilya Morozov, and Stephan Seiler · 2026 · The RAND Journal of Economics

    Learned representations of unstructured product data as inputs to a demand model.

  2. Mostly Harmless Machine Learning: Learning Optimal Instruments in Linear IV Models
    Jiafeng Chen, Daniel L. Chen, and Greg Lewis · 2021 working-paper version

    Machine learning and sample splitting for instrument construction within a linear structural equation.

  3. Efficient Estimation of Average Derivatives in NPIV Models: Simulation Comparisons of Neural Network Estimators
    Jiafeng Chen, Xiaohong Chen, and Elie Tamer · 2023 · Journal of Econometrics

    Neural-network estimators for nonparametric IV models, with attention to inference and finite-sample performance.

  4. Dynamic Spatial Competition in Early Education: An Equilibrium Analysis of the Preschool Market in Pennsylvania
    Pierre Bodéré · Working paper

    A dynamic competition application connecting economic equilibrium, neural approximation, and policy evaluation.

  5. Market Counterfactuals with Nonparametric Supply: An ML/AI Approach
    Harold D. Chiang, Jack Collison, Lorenzo Magnolfi, and Christopher Sullivan · 2026 working paper

    Flexible supply estimation with instruments, followed by equilibrium counterfactual analysis.

  6. Deep Neural Networks for Estimation and Inference
    Max H. Farrell, Tengyuan Liang, and Sanjog Misra · 2021 · Econometrica

    A framework for using deep-learning estimates in semiparametric inference, with a direct-mail marketing application.
